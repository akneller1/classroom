Teacher Guide

Everything on this site is free, from Grade 1 to Calculus, and needs no accounts. This guide explains how to find your unit, make printables, choose a game, run an Interactive Math lesson, plan the year and send students a link.

Quick start

Most visits take three steps.

  1. Pick a courseOn the home page, choose a grade or course and what you need. On any other page, use Choose your course at the top.
  2. Open a unitA unit page holds everything for that unit: the generators, games, study guides and Interactive Math.
  3. Make, play or shareBuild a lesson, worksheet, printable or assessment, play a game, or copy a student link.

Grades 1–5

1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th and 5th Grade Math, each following the Common Core domains for that grade.

Grades 6–8

6th Grade Math, 7th Grade Math (accelerated Grade 7) and 8th Grade Math.

High school

Algebra 1, Geometry, Algebra 2, Precalculus, Calculus, Statistics, Discrete Math and Financial Algebra.

7th Grade Math is the accelerated Grade 7 course: it covers Grade 7 and much of Grade 8. For a standard Grade 7 sequence, use the Grade 7 standards hub.

Making resources

The generators build fresh problems every time. They share a few options.

Options every generator shares
  • Level. Three levels set the difficulty of every problem: Support, Standard and Advanced. High-school menus also show the track code, for example Support (CP2), Standard (CP1) and Advanced (Honors).
  • Skills. Tick the unit's skills you want. Skills that don't suit a format are greyed out, and the reason is shown.
  • Versions. Up to three versions (A, B, C) with different numbers, which helps against copying.
  • Student copy and answer key. Switch between the two views and print each one separately.
  • New problems. Makes new problems with the same settings.

The site checks the answers to the problems it generates. If you spot a mistake, use Report a problem on the card or Feedback or report an issue at the bottom of any page. Include the link and what is wrong.

Lesson Plan

Builds a full day's lesson from the course's scope and sequence: objective, vocabulary, Do Now, notes, worked examples, practice and exit ticket.

  • Course, unit and day. Choose them at the top. On lesson days you can tick other lessons in the unit to combine their problems.
  • Student notes. Completed notes fill everything in. Guided notes leave key words and answers blank. The teacher key always prints completed.
  • Include in lesson. Untick a section to leave it out. You can edit section minutes and notes, and your edits are saved in your browser.
  • Output. Print the student handout or the teacher key, or download the lesson as a PowerPoint.
Worksheet

Makes a practice worksheet for any unit, mixing multiple-choice and show-your-work problems. Choose the skills, the level and the number of versions. The answer key shows worked solutions.

Problems that depend on a picture (a graph, a shape, a number line, a fraction model) print with that picture on the student copy.

Mazes & Puzzles (printables and projects)

The Mazes & Puzzles shortcut opens the printable generator. Pick a printable on the left, then a level and skills:

  • Maze: each correct answer leads to the next room.
  • Color by Number: answers map to colors that reveal a picture.
  • Tarsia Puzzle: students cut out triangles and match problems to answers.
  • Error Analysis: find, explain and fix a student's mistake.
  • Partner Check: Partner A and Partner B solve different problems that have the same answers.
  • Whodunnit: clue cards to post around the room, and a case board to solve.
  • Stations: one page per station, plus an answer key.
  • Matching Card Sort (function units): match graph, equation, table and description cards. Play online opens the digital Matching Game.
  • Vocabulary Builder: matching, definition, diagram and crossword sheets with the unit's vocabulary.
  • Projects: 2–3 day projects in three levels, as PDF, Word or PowerPoint, with a teacher guide.

Not every format is offered in every course: the list on the left shows what that course has. Some units also have ready-made PDF versions of these printables as cards on the unit page.

Assessment
  • Formats. A standard unit test (multiple choice and short answer), or a choice-menu assessment where students pick problems to reach a point total.
  • Settings. Choose skills, level, number of versions and point values.
  • Editing. On the review screen you can replace, move, delete or edit any problem, or write your own.
  • Your own problems. The site can't check an answer it didn't generate. Tick I checked this answer on the problem card to approve it before printing.

Saved assessments stay in your browser. Download one as a file to keep it or move it to another computer.

Lesson ideas that use several pieces
  • New topic: Lesson Plan with guided notes → an Interactive Math tool or animated diagram for the exploration → Worksheet for homework.
  • Review day: Stations or a Maze in class → a projected Showdown or Math Millionaire to finish → the cheat sheet for home.
  • Before a test: students take the readiness check from a student link, then practice the skills it flags with flashcards or a solo game.

Which game when

The games depend on the grade band. Within a band, every unit has the same set, and every game asks you to choose skills and a level before it starts. Each game card carries a label: Individual Review Solo or Team Games Team Games or Collaborative Challenges.

New: solo play. Connect 4, Tic-Tac-Toe and Four in a Row used to be two-team games only. Each now also has Solo vs. computer: one student answers a problem to earn each move, and the computer takes its own turns at Easy, Medium or Hard. A student link always opens solo play. Tic-Tac-Toe is also new in Grades 1–5.

Post-it games (all grades)

Quick sorting, matching and ordering games for the board or a projector, for example Rounding Rush in Grade 3 or Slope Match in Grade 8. Each one runs from Easy to Expert. They are listed with the unit's games, and Set up student link sends one to students.

To project a game, open it on the teacher computer and use your browser's full-screen mode (F11 on Windows, Control-Command-F on a Mac). If the back row can't read it, zoom in with Ctrl or Cmd and +.

Animated diagrams (new)

These show a mathematical idea in motion. Each has Play/Pause, Start and End buttons, a speed setting and a slider to step through it by hand. If a device is set to reduce motion, Play jumps straight to the end.

  • 3D Nets: Fold and Unfold (Grades 6–8, Geometry): nets fold into a cube, prisms, a pyramid, a tetrahedron and a cylinder, and back. Drag the solid to turn it. A 2 × 3 block shows a net that doesn't fold into a cube.
  • Circle Area: Unroll the Sectors (Grade 7, Geometry): the sectors of a circle line up into a shape πr wide and r tall.
  • Pythagorean Theorem: A Proof in Motion (Grades 7–8, Geometry): four triangles rearrange inside a square to show c² = a² + b².
  • Triangle Angle Sum: Tear and Line Up (Grades 7–8, Geometry): the three corners of any triangle make a straight angle.
  • Volume by Pouring (Grades 7–8, Geometry): three cones fill a cylinder; a sphere fills two thirds of one.
  • Make a Ten: Regrouping with Base-Ten Blocks (Grades 1–3): ten ones become a ten when adding, and a ten opens into ten ones when subtracting.
What comes with each tool?
  • The tool: sliders, draggable points, models or an animation. Points can be dragged with a mouse, a finger or the arrow keys.
  • A "Try this" list: a few challenges that check themselves and turn green when a student completes one.
  • An Explore & Reflect worksheet: a printable PDF with a teacher key, linked from the tool's card. Most keys end with teacher notes: the intended discovery, likely misconceptions, and which answers can vary.
  • A "What should I notice?" panel on most 6–12 tools. It stays closed until a student opens it, so it doesn't give the discovery away.
  • ▣ Present: a larger projector layout, full screen where the browser allows, with zoom buttons and Fit.
How do I run a lesson with one?
  1. Print the Explore & Reflect worksheet from the tool's card.
  2. Predict: students answer before they open the tool. Wrong predictions are fine; they make the next step stick.
  3. Explore: students open the tool from a student link, or you project it with ▣ Present, and they fill in the tables.
  4. Explain: sentence frames help students state the rule they found, for example "Changing b moves the line up or down."
  5. Apply: students answer new questions without the tool, as a quick check that the rule transfers.

The exploration usually takes 15–20 minutes, and the whole worksheet fits in one class period. The "Try this" list works well as a warm-up or for early finishers.

Some Statistics labs draw random samples. Add &seed= and a number to the end of the copied student link (for example &seed=2024) and every student gets the same random results, which makes whole-class comparisons easy.
Projecting a tool or animation for the whole class? Have students call out a prediction before you move a slider or press Play, then reveal the result.

Sharing with students

Give students a link to one activity, without the rest of the site.

How do I send students to one game, tool or study guide?
  1. Find the activity on its unit page or in Browse activities.
  2. Click Copy student link (or Set up student link) on its card.
  3. For most games, a window opens first. Choose the skills and level students should practice, then copy the link.
  4. Paste the link into Google Classroom, Canvas, Teams or an email.

The link opens only that activity, with only the skills you chose. Students don't see the site's menus, the generators or the answer keys. A link to Connect 4, Tic-Tac-Toe or Four in a Row always opens solo play against the computer.

Which activities have a student link?

Anything a student can use alone: flashcards, cheat sheets, the readiness check, Graphing Practice, Interactive Math tools, and every game labeled Individual Review, Solo or Team Games or Collaborative Challenges. That includes Math Millionaire, Escape Room, Detective, Picture Reveal and Asteroid Blaster, and in Grades 1–5 every game except the team races.

Games labeled Team Games (Showdown; Rocket Race, Math Race and Math Soccer in Grades 1–5) don't have one, because they are meant to run from the teacher's screen. Printables don't either: print them or share the PDF.

How do I give students the readiness check?

On the unit page, use Copy student link on the Practice Test card (Grades 6–12) or the Understanding Check card (Grades 1–5). In Grades 6–12 a setup window opens first, where you choose:

  • the level
  • the number of questions (5–25)
  • a time limit (none, 20, 30 or 45 minutes)
  • the answer format (mixed, multiple choice, or short answer)
  • the skills

Students take it online and get worked solutions and skill-by-skill feedback right away. Then they can practice what they missed with new questions.

How is a short answer graded?
  • Graded automatically: numbers, ordered pairs, solutions, expressions and many word answers. Equal forms count (½ and 0.5, $1.25 and 125¢, 3:30 and half past 3), and students can type exponents with a caret, as in x^2 or m^2. A unit is optional, but a different unit is marked wrong.
  • Checked by the student: answers the site can't judge on its own (a written explanation, a form the question asks for). The student compares their work with the worked solution and a short checklist, then marks it right or wrong. If the last line of their answer matches the key exactly, it is marked correct for them.
Can students get from a student link to the rest of the site?

Not by clicking: a student link has no menus or links back to the site. It is a focused view, not a password, so a student who edits the web address by hand could still reach the public pages.

Answer keys only appear when you build or print them from a generator, and they are never part of a student link. Keep printed keys and key PDFs out of shared folders.

Planning the year

Every course has a pacing guide, from 1st Grade Math to Financial Algebra.

  • Where: the Pacing Guide shortcut on any course or unit page, the Planning link on the course overview pages, or search for "pacing".
  • What's in it: a 180-day plan in two semesters, a one-semester (90-day block) plan, and a table of which skills practice each standard. Every lesson links to a ready plan in the Lesson Planner.
  • Spring testing: Grades 3–8 plans teach every unit before the spring MCAS, then add review and testing days. Grades 1 and 2 have no state test, so the same window holds spring review and a school or district benchmark. Calculus and Statistics plans are built around the AP exam.

FAQ

Do students or teachers need an account?

No. There is nothing to sign up for, and the site collects no information about students. The privacy page explains the anonymous visit counts the site keeps.

Where are my saved activities and edits stored?

In the browser you used: saved activities, lesson-plan edits and saved assessments. They don't follow you to another computer or browser, and clearing browsing data removes them. Use Back Up Saved Work to download a backup and restore it on another computer.

Why does a game ask me to choose skills when I open it?

So one game can cover a whole unit or just today's lesson. Choose the skills and level, then start. A student link remembers the skills and level you picked.

Can one student play a board game like Connect 4 alone?

Yes. Connect 4, Tic-Tac-Toe and Four in a Row have Solo vs. computer as well as two teams. In solo play the student answers a problem to earn each move, there are no steals, and the computer plays at Easy, Medium or Hard. It takes its wins, blocks yours and plans a few moves ahead (more on Hard), but it doesn't always choose the best move and it sometimes misses its own problem, so a student who answers carefully can win. A student link always opens solo play.

The levels have different names in different places. Are they the same?

Yes. Every course uses the same three levels: Support, Standard and Advanced. High-school teacher menus also show the matching track code, as in Support (CP2), Standard (CP1) and Advanced (Honors). Games played against the computer have their own Easy, Medium and Hard, which set how well the computer plays, and Post-it games run from Easy to Expert.

Something printed badly or an answer looks wrong. What do I do?

For printing, use the generator's own Print buttons. They set the page size and margins, so turn off "Fit to page" in the print dialog.

For a wrong answer, use Report a problem on the activity's card, or Feedback or report an issue at the bottom of any page. Include the link and the problem, and it will be fixed.

Which browsers work?

Any current version of Chrome, Edge, Safari or Firefox, on computers, Chromebooks and tablets. Phones work for flashcards, games and Interactive Math; the generators are easier on a larger screen.
