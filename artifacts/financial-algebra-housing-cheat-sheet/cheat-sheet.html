6Cash needed to move

  • Down payment + closing costs + moving costs.
  • Keep emergency reserves after closing.
  • Renting also may need a deposit, first month’s rent and moving costs.
  • A refundable deposit ties up cash but is not a permanent cost if fully returned.
  • Check which fees and deposits are refundable; do not double-count them.

1Which home option wins?

One-year model: rent $1,800 monthly. Buy with $2,500 monthly outflow, $4,000 of principal repaid, $6,000 nonrefundable transaction costs.
Example: No price change, tax benefit or opportunity cost; no other costs.
Rent cost: 1,800(12)=21,6001{,}800(12)=21{,}6001,800(12)=21,600
Buy net cost: 2,500(12)−4,000+6,000=32,0002{,}500(12)-4{,}000+6{,}000=32{,}0002,500(12)−4,000+6,000=32,000
Answer: Rent costs $10,400 less here

2What changes the answer?

The short-horizon comparison favors renting because transaction costs have little time to spread out.
  • Longer stay can spread buying and selling costs.
  • Higher rent growth can favor buying.
  • Repairs, price declines and investing the down payment can favor renting.
  • Space, stability and flexibility may matter more than the modeled dollars.
Run several plausible scenarios.

4Down-payment alternative

Opportunity cost = growth forgone on money used elsewhere. Hypothetical constant return; no tax or fees.
Example: $70,000 could earn 4% annually for 3 years.

5Compare on the same basis

Over the same horizon, total cash paid before sale (down payment, closing and monthly outflows) minus net sale proceeds gives a basic net cost.
Net sale proceeds = sale price − remaining mortgage − sale costs.
If sale costs are already subtracted here, do not add them a second time.
Add opportunity cost consistently, or compare full ending net worth. Do not mix the two methods.

6Reminders & mistakes

✔ Do remember
  • Use the same horizon and housing quality.
  • Principal repayment builds equity but still uses monthly cash.
✘ Common mistakes
  • Rent is always wasted: it buys housing and flexibility.
  • Prices always rise: test flat and falling values.
  • Mortgage = cost of owning: add tax, insurance and repairs.
  • Count initial equity as profit: subtract the cash invested.
