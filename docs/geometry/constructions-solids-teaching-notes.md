# Geometry · New skills — teaching notes

## Foundations of Geometry · Skill 9 · Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions (G-CO.D.12, G-CO.D.13)

**Big idea.** A construction is a proof you can see. The compass copies a distance, so every construction works because it creates congruent segments. The congruent segments make triangles that are congruent by SSS.

| Construction | Key compass move | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Copy a segment | Opening = AB | Radius = AB |
| Copy an angle | Two settings: the arc, then the chord between its crossings | SSS → the angles are congruent |
| Perpendicular bisector | Opening > ½AB, same from A and B | The crossings are equidistant from A and B |
| Angle bisector | Arc from the vertex, then equal arcs from both crossings | SSS → the two halves are congruent |
| Perpendicular through P | Arc from P cuts the line twice; equal arcs from those points | P and the new point are both equidistant from the two cuts |
| Parallel through P | Copy the corresponding angle at P | Congruent corresponding angles → parallel lines |
| Equilateral triangle on AB | Radius AB from both A and B | AC = BC = AB |
| Regular hexagon in a circle | Step the radius around the circle 6 times | 6 equilateral triangles, each with a 60° central angle |

**Common errors (used as distractors).** Changing the compass opening between the two arcs; using half of AB for the perpendicular bisector, so the arcs don't meet; thinking the hexagon side is half the radius; mixing up "copy an angle" (two settings) with "bisect an angle".

**Construction Lab (printable activity, 25 min).** Each student needs a compass, straightedge and one sheet. Stations:
1. Bisect segment AB = 9 cm; measure AM.
2. Bisect a 110° angle; measure both halves.
3. Copy ∠A at D, then check the copy with a protractor.
4. Construct a hexagon in a circle of radius 4 cm; find the perimeter.

Exit question: "Which construction would you use to find the point on a field that is equally far from two goalposts? Explain." (Answer: the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining them.)

## Area, Surface Area and Volume · Skill 11 · Cross-Sections and Solids of Rotation (G-GMD.B.4, G-GMD.A.1)

**Big idea.** Slicing a solid with a plane gives a 2-D cross-section. Rotating a 2-D figure about an axis gives a 3-D solid.

- **Cube:** triangle, square, rectangle, pentagon or hexagon are all possible; a circle is never possible.
- **Cylinder:** circle (parallel to the base), rectangle (through the axis), or ellipse (tilted).
- **Cone:** circle, ellipse, or triangle (through the vertex).
- **Sphere:** always a circle.
- **Rotation:** a rectangle → cylinder; a right triangle about a leg → cone; a half-disk about its diameter → sphere.
- **Cavalieri:** if two solids have the same height and equal cross-sectional areas at every height, they have equal volume (the leaning coin stack).

**Hands-on.** Use clay or play-dough and dental floss to slice a cube, cylinder and cone; or spin paper cut-outs taped to a pencil to "see" the solid of rotation.

## Area, Surface Area and Volume · Skill 12 · Modeling with Density and Design (G-MG.A.1–3)

**Big idea.** Density is a rate: amount per unit of space. Population density = people ÷ area, and mass density = mass ÷ volume. Design problems combine area or volume with a constraint and a cost.

**Worked example.** A solid copper cube has 3 cm edges, and copper's density is 8.96 g/cm³. Mass = 8.96 × 27 ≈ 242 g.

**Worked example (design).** An open box is made from a 12 in square sheet by cutting x-in squares from the corners. V(x) = (12 − 2x)²x. Checking x = 1…5 gives V = 100, 128, 108, 64, 20, so x = 2 in gives the greatest volume.

**Common errors.** Dividing area by population; forgetting to cube the edge length; rounding the number of paint quarts down; using the full sheet side (instead of S − 2x) for the box base.

**Project hook.** "Design the cheapest cylindrical can that holds 355 mL": students compare surface area for several radii, building on the open-box pattern.
